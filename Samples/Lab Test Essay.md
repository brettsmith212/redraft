# The Case for Writing Slowly

Most of us write the way we eat lunch at our desks: quickly, distractedly, and without much thought about what we just consumed. We open a document, we type until the cursor stops moving, and then we hit send. It is efficient, in a sense. But I have come to believe that the speed at which we write is quietly costing us something important, and that the fix is not a new tool or a clever technique but simply the willingness to slow down.

## Speed is not the same as clarity

There is a common belief that a fast writer is a good writer. We admire people who can dash off a memo in five minutes, and we envy the colleague who answers every email before lunch. But speed and clarity are not the same thing, and in my experience they are often at odds. When I write fast, I write what I think I mean, which is not always what I actually mean, and the gap between those two things is where almost every misunderstanding I have ever caused has come from, whether it was a project brief that sent a team in the wrong direction for a week or a short note to a friend that landed in a way I never intended and took a long phone call to repair.

The first draft is a conversation with yourself. It is messy, repetitive, and full of half-finished thoughts. That is fine. The problem is when the first draft becomes the final draft, which, frankly, is basically what happens most of the time, because we are in a hurry and the words look roughly correct on the screen and nobody has time to really go back and actually read them again.

## What slowing down actually looks like

Slowing down does not mean writing less. It does not mean agonizing over every comma or spending an hour on a two-line reply. It means building in a pause between writing and sending, and using that pause to read what you wrote as if someone else had written it.

When I do this, I notice things. I notice that I used the word "really" four times in one paragraph. I notice that a sentence I thought was elegant is actually just long. I notice that the most important point is buried in the third paragraph, after two paragraphs of throat-clearing that exist mostly because I was warming up. I notice that I wrote "leverage our synergies going forward" when what I meant was "work together."

These are small things. But small things add up. A reader who has to work to understand you will eventually stop trying, and a reader who stops trying is a reader you have lost.

## The tyranny of the blank page, and its opposite

People talk a lot about the tyranny of the blank page, the paralysis that comes from not knowing how to begin. I understand that feeling. But I think there is an opposite problem that gets far less attention, which is the tyranny of the full page: the false confidence that comes from having written a lot of words, and the reluctance to cut any of them because cutting feels like admitting that the time spent writing them was wasted, even though the time was not wasted at all, since writing the bad version is often the only way to discover the good one, and the good one is almost always shorter.

Cutting is the most underrated skill in writing. Every editor I have ever worked with has told me some version of the same thing: your piece is better with a third of it gone. They were right every single time, lol. It hurt, but they were right.

## Alternatives are a form of thinking

One habit that has helped me enormously is keeping alternatives. When I am not sure whether a sentence should say "difficult" or "hard," or whether a paragraph should open with a question or a statement, I write both versions and keep them side by side. Then I come back later, with fresh eyes, and choose.

This sounds inefficient, and in a narrow sense it is. But it turns indecision into something useful. Instead of agonizing in the moment, I defer the decision until I know more. Often the right choice is obvious an hour later. Sometimes neither version survives and a third one appears that is better than both. Either way, the act of writing alternatives forces me to articulate what I am actually choosing between, which is itself a form of thinking.

## Reading aloud

The single most effective thing I have ever done to improve my writing is read it out loud. Not in my head, out loud, with my actual voice, ideally to an empty room so nobody thinks I have lost it.

Reading aloud catches everything. It catches sentences that run on too long, because you run out of breath. It catches awkward rhythms, because your tongue trips over them. It catches words that sound pompous, because you hear yourself saying "heretofore" and immediately want to crawl under the desk. It catches repetition, because hearing the same word three times in a row is far more grating than seeing it.

If a sentence is hard to say, it is almost certainly hard to read. Fix it.

## Writing for the reader, not for yourself

Underneath all of this is a simple shift in perspective. When we write fast, we write for ourselves. We put down what is in our heads in the order it occurs to us, and we assume the reader will follow along. When we write slowly, we write for the reader. We ask what they already know, what they need to know, and what order will make the most sense to someone who is not inside our heads.

This is harder. It requires a kind of empathy that does not come naturally when you are rushing. You have to imagine being someone else, reading your words for the first time, without any of the context you take for granted. You have to ask whether the point is clear, whether the example helps, whether the conclusion follows from what came before or just sort of shows up because you ran out of things to say and needed to wrap it up somehow before the meeting started.

But the reward is enormous. Clear writing is a gift to the reader. It saves them time, it respects their attention, and it makes them more likely to actually do whatever it is you are asking them to do.

## A modest proposal

So here is what I am proposing, and it is not complicated. Write your first draft as fast as you like. Then stop. Walk away, even for five minutes. Come back and read it as a stranger would. Cut what does not need to be there. Read it aloud. Choose between your alternatives. And only then, when it says exactly what you mean and nothing more, send it.

It will take a little longer. It will be worth it. Your readers will notice, even if they never say so, and you will notice too, in the slow and satisfying way that good work always reveals itself over time.
